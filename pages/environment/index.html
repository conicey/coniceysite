Environment

Extinction

Human environmental impacts are pushing the natural world toward a silent, irreversible catastrophe: around 1 million animal and plant species are now threatened with extinction, many within decades, while more than 500,000 terrestrial species already lack enough habitat to survive long-term without restoration. Habitat destruction, pollution, climate change, overexploitation, and invasive species are not distant problems—they are stripping animals of the food, shelter, and space they need, turning once-thriving ecosystems into empty landscapes. Every species lost is permanent: no campaign, apology, or replacement can bring back an animal erased from Earth. If this destruction continues, future generations will inherit a poorer, quieter planet, where the diversity of life has been reduced to photographs, museum specimens, and regret.

Deforestation

The timber industry often boasts that it plants four trees for every one it cuts down, but this promise hides an ugly truth: a tree is not a forest. Natural forests are complex, living systems built up over centuries, while industrial plantations replace them with rigid, unnatural columns of one fast-growing species chosen for profit rather than ecology. These monoculture farms support far less wildlife, store less carbon, and provide weaker protection against erosion and drought than the native forests they displace. In some cases, tree-planting schemes have even accelerated biodiversity loss by subsidizing the replacement of biologically rich native forests with commercial plantations. Calling this “reforestation” is misleading: it is not restoring nature—it is swapping an irreplaceable ecosystem for a sterile wood factory.

Global Warming

Global warming is not a distant warning—it is an unfolding emergency caused by humanity’s relentless release of greenhouse gases, which has already heated the planet by about 1.1 degrees Celsius since the late 1800s. The consequences are becoming impossible to ignore: longer and deadlier heat waves, more severe droughts, destructive wildfires, heavier floods, and more intense storms are all increasing as the climate warms. Melting glaciers and ice sheets, combined with warming oceans, are raising sea levels and swallowing coastlines, while marine heat waves and acidification are devastating coral reefs and the creatures that depend on them. If warming passes 1.5 degrees Celsius, the damage becomes far worse and, in many cases, irreversible—coral reefs could decline by 70 to 90 percent, and ice-sheet loss could eventually drive multi-metre sea-level rise. This is the price of treating the atmosphere as a dumping ground: a hotter, less stable world where the systems that sustain human life are pushed toward collapse.

Corporate Greed
Nestlé

Nestlé’s environmental impact is far larger because of its global food, beverage, packaging, and agricultural supply chains. The company reported withdrawing 96.1 million cubic metres of water in 2025, and its bottled-water business has faced repeated criticism and community opposition over groundwater extraction, including claims that extraction can lower water tables and affect local ecosystems during droughts. Nestlé has also been repeatedly identified by the Break Free From Plastic coalition as one of the world’s top plastic polluters, and it reported using 1.7 million tonnes of plastic packaging in 2018, much of it single-use. In addition, its supply chains for palm oil, cocoa, coffee, and other commodities carry deforestation and land-use risks, adding to its greenhouse-gas emissions and biodiversity impact.

Montecarlo International

Monte Carlo Fashions has a significant environmental footprint because apparel production is water- and energy-intensive. In FY 2023–24, the company withdrew and consumed 65,542 kilolitres of groundwater, and it discharged 37,991 kilolitres of water, including 25,639 kilolitres into surface water. Its textile operations also generate wastewater, fabric waste, and dependence on synthetic fibres such as polyester, which are made from fossil fuels and shed microplastics. While the company reports treatment and reuse systems, its continued large-scale groundwater extraction and wastewater discharge mean its manufacturing still places pressure on local water resources.
